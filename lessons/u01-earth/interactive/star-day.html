Why noon-to-noon is a little longer than one spin

Earth spins and moves around the Sun at the same time. Watch the red dot: when does it point at the far-away star again, and when does it point at the Sun again?

Seen from above the North Pole (not to scale)

  • Play the 12-day year until the dot points at the star again. Is it noon yet?
  • Count over one whole year: how many spins, and how many noons?
  • Switch to the real Earth. How big is the difference now?

A sidereal day ("star day") is one spin measured against the far-away stars: about 23 hours 56 minutes for Earth. A solar day is measured against the Sun, from one local noon to the next: about 24 hours. The solar day is longer because Earth has moved along its orbit, so it must turn a little extra to face the Sun again. Source: OpenStax Astronomy 2e 4.3. A clock here counts 24-hour solar days; the 12-day year is a made-up "what if".
