Foucault's pendulum: catching the Earth turning

In 1851 Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball on a very long wire in Paris. Hours later, its swing pointed a different way. Here's how it worked, and how you can check the idea at home.

Side view: the Panthéon, Paris, 1851

Swing
  1. A very long wire (about 67 m, as tall as a 20-storey building) hangs from the top of the dome. A long wire makes a slow, steady swing that keeps going for hours.
  2. A heavy ball (about 28 kg, or 62 lb) hangs at the bottom. Its weight keeps air and small bumps from changing its swing.
  3. The start: the ball was pulled to one side and let go carefully, so it swung back and forth in one straight line, with no sideways push.
  4. The marks: a pointer under the ball traced a path in a thin layer of sand on the floor. Every swing left its mark a tiny bit turned from the one before.
  5. Before Paris: Foucault first tried it with a short pendulum in his own basement, then showed it at the Paris Observatory on February 3, 1851, before the big public show at the Panthéon that March.

Looking down on the floor

Look from
  • Predict: at the equator, how long until the first peg falls? Then try it.
  • Switch to "the stars" and choose the North Pole. What is really moving: the swing, or the floor?
  • Choose Sydney. Which way do the pegs fall now?

Lazy-Susan pendulum (seen from above)

  1. You need: a lazy Susan or a spinning office chair, a small stand or a chair-back to hang from, string about 50 cm long, and a weight (a bag of coins, a heavy washer, or a small bottle of water).
  2. Tie the weight to the string and hang it from the stand. Put the stand in the middle of the turntable.
  3. Pull the weight to one side and let go gently, so it swings in a straight line toward, say, the door.
  4. Slowly turn the turntable. Watch the stand turn, but the swing keeps pointing at the door.
  5. Now imagine you're a tiny bug standing on the turntable. To you, the swing seems to turn. Earth is the turntable, and we are the bugs.

This home version only shows the idea: you turned the table yourself. Foucault's pendulum is real evidence because nobody turned anything, yet the swing still turned, by the amount the Earth's spin predicts for Paris.

Facts about the 1851 Panthéon pendulum: wire about 67 m (220 ft) and ball about 28 kg (62 lb), per APS News and Britannica (OpenStax rounds these to 60 m and 25 kg). One swing there and back takes about 16 seconds, worked out from the wire's length. The swing turns by 360° × sin(latitude) in one star day (23.9 hours): a full circle in about 24 hours at the poles, about 32 hours in Paris (about 11° an hour), and never at the equator. Sources: OpenStax Astronomy 2e 4.1; APS News, "February 3, 1851: Léon Foucault Demonstrates That Earth Rotates"; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Foucault pendulum".
